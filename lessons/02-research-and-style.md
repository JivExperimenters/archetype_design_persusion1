**You will learn:** how historical evidence becomes a design choice.

## Research an object, not a search thumbnail

1. Open a museum, archive, or authoritative collection record.
2. Record creator, title, date, institution, direct URL, and image rights.
3. Describe something you can actually see.
4. Explain how to adapt that feature to a hero.
5. Separate your observation from the institution's interpretation.

**Example record:** Josef Müller-Brockmann, *Musica Viva*, 1957, photolithograph, Museum of Modern Art, object 58.1960. [View the record](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/4885). Follow its licensing guidance; this tutorial links to the work rather than reproducing it.

**Example record:** Ettore Sottsass, *“Carlton” Room Divider*, 1981, wood and plastic laminate, The Met, object 1997.460.1a–d. The record identifies Memphis Milano as manufacturer and credits the estate for rights. [View the record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/486989).

The artifact is primary evidence. Museum interpretation provides context. Your generated hero is a new design, not a historical source.

## Two approaches to a hero

Both modernism and postmodernism contain multiple movements. Here we compare **Swiss/International Typographic Style-inspired organization** with a **Memphis-inspired postmodern treatment**. “Modern” does not simply mean current, and “postmodern” does not mean any colorful page.

| Choice | Swiss-inspired approach | Memphis-inspired approach |
|---|---|---|
| Organization | Repeated alignments and a clear grid | Asymmetry, overlap, playful geometry |
| Typography | Strong scale hierarchy; restrained type choices | Expressive display type; readable supporting text |
| Imagery | Deliberate rectangular crop or disciplined placement | Cutout, collage, tilted panel, unexpected juxtaposition |
| CTA | Distinct block aligned with copy | Quiet, legible target in an expressive composition |

These are application suggestions. See the [V&A's postmodernism discussion](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/what-is-postmodernism) for its challenge to modernist ideals of clarity and simplicity, and [MoMA's Bauhaus design account](https://production-gcp.moma.org/explore/inside_out/2009/11/06/bauhaus-the-graphic-design-department-goes-back-to-school/) for an institutional example of grid-led typography.

## Translate; do not merely decorate

**Weak:** “I used bright colors because this is postmodern.”

**Stronger:** “I adapted the angled forms and contrasting blocks I observed in Carlton into layered panels. I kept the headline and CTA on a quiet surface so the offer remains readable.”

Furniture can inform color and composition; it is not evidence for a historical web layout. State that cross-medium adaptation.

Modernist designs can use strong color and illustration. Postmodern designs can use photography and clear hierarchy. Your historical argument depends on specific choices and sources.

## Try it in your research page

For each example, finish: **“I observe ____. I will adapt it by ____. This helps the visitor ____.”**

Sketch identical headline/CTA content using repeated alignment, then controlled overlap. Keep the CTA clear in both.

**Carry forward:** these observations become the source explanations beneath your heroes.
