## Begin with inquiry

The Sage archetype is built around knowledge, truth, and understanding. A Sage brand does not simply announce that it is an expert; it shows how it knows, explains what the evidence can support, and makes room for the audience to think.

This approach may appeal to people who value research, learning, careful decisions, and credible expertise. Use it when the goal is to help an audience understand a subject—not overwhelm them with jargon or ask them to trust an unsupported claim.

## A Sage page: inquiry → insight → evidence → reflection

[Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/) offers a useful example of research-led web communication. It organizes complex topics into readable explanations, visual evidence, and links to data and methods. Borrow that sequence for your own Sage page:

### Choose type for clarity

Try [**Source Serif 4**](https://fonts.google.com/specimen/Source+Serif+4) for considered, editorial headlines and [**Source Sans 3**](https://fonts.google.com/specimen/Source+Sans+3) for readable paragraphs, captions, and buttons. The serif headline adds a reflective, scholarly quality; the sans serif keeps evidence and navigation direct. Use a clear size hierarchy and comfortable line spacing. These are suggested fonts, not a claim about any institution's proprietary type.

This is sample framing, not a research finding. Before publishing a real claim, cite the study or institution that supports it, include dates or context when relevant, and summarize what the evidence can—and cannot—show. A chart should help readers inspect the evidence, not decorate an unsupported conclusion.

## Design styles

### Swiss / International Typographic Style

This style uses grids, sans-serif type, careful spacing, and clear hierarchy to make information easier to follow. For a Sage page, those choices can make evidence feel organized and approachable: align the headline, explanation, source note, and CTA so readers can see how they relate.

### Deconstruction

Deconstruction questions familiar visual conventions through overlap, fragmentation, and unexpected relationships between words and images. It can suit a Sage who is examining assumptions or comparing perspectives. Keep the central claim, its evidence, and the next step legible; experimentation should prompt thought, not hide the reasoning.

## Persuasion principle: Authority

Authority is most useful when readers can inspect its basis. Name the qualified expert or institution, link to the source, and show the evidence behind the claim. A Sage design should help people judge credibility for themselves—not rely on a badge or confident tone alone.

## Apply it to a hero

- **Swiss-inspired:** Use a consistent grid, a strong headline, concise explanatory copy, a sourced evidence block, and a clearly labeled next step.
- **Deconstruction-inspired:** Layer a visual or quotation to invite a second look, then anchor the composition with a readable claim, a visible source, and a clear next step.

In both versions, show the source beside the claim it supports. Distinguish **what the source reports** from **your interpretation**. If the evidence is incomplete, say so; a wise design makes uncertainty visible instead of disguising it.
